## Designing Collaborative Sensemaking with Concept Mapping

*Collaborative learning design · Online undergraduate Information Science course*

**Context & Design Challenge**

Students needed support in understanding how complex systems-design concepts relate to one another and in articulating those relationships during group work.

**My Role**

Designed a two-step concept mapping activity, including learner instructions, group collaboration guidelines, and requirements for the shared map.

**Design Approach & Rationale**

Students first developed individual concept maps, then met in groups of three to negotiate and integrate their ideas into a shared map using CmapTools. Requiring learners to label conceptual relationships made the reasoning behind their connections explicit. Individual preparation gave each learner a starting point for collaborative sensemaking.

**Evidence & Reflection**

The project documentation reports active comparison and integration of ideas, with richer conversations in groups with balanced preparation. Possible refinements include reducing preparatory workload, providing guiding questions for negotiation, and incorporating a brief individual reflection.

## Structuring High-Quality Online Discussions with Assigned Roles

*Collaborative learning design · Introduction to Business Information Systems, Penn State World Campus*

**Context & Design Challenge**

In a fully online undergraduate course, asynchronous team discussions could involve uneven participation and surface-level agreement. The design challenge was to support productive interaction within the existing course project.

**My Role**

Designed research-informed, role-structured discussion modules that were formally accepted into the course curriculum.

**Design Approach & Rationale**

Assigned distinct interactional responsibilities to five collaborative roles: Moderator, Summarizer, Skeptic, Source Searcher, and Theoretician. These responsibilities scaffolded coordination, synthesis, critique, evidence sharing, and connections to theoretical concepts. The roles were embedded in an existing team project while keeping the case, content, and deliverables consistent across role-assigned and unstructured conditions.

**Evidence & Reflection**

The work-sample documentation describes patterns of participation, synthesis, critique, and information sharing in role-assigned teams. Ongoing research examines how roles are enacted and how interaction differs between conditions. These observations inform refinements to role instructions and the support provided for roles with different cognitive demands; they do not establish that assigned roles improve every learning outcome.

## Data-Informed Group Formation to Support Seminar Collaboration

*Data-informed learning design · Seminar for Engineering Teaching Assistants, Penn State University*

**Context & Design Challenge**

Informally formed groups in a graduate-level hybrid seminar could exhibit uneven participation, with some members contributing substantially more than others. The design challenge was to use evidence of existing interaction to inform group formation.

**My Role**

As a teaching assistant, designed and implemented a network-informed approach to organizing groups for asynchronous discussions and collaborative seminar activities.

**Design Approach & Rationale**

Used early discussion interactions to construct an interaction network and applied the Louvain community detection algorithm to identify existing clusters. These results informed group assignments that considered participants with different levels of network centrality and helped avoid isolating quieter learners. Stable groups supported subsequent discussions, peer feedback, and mini-projects.

**Evidence & Reflection**

The project produced network-informed group assignments, network visualizations, and a repeatable grouping process. The work sample reports observations of more distributed participation and stronger reciprocity. Potential refinements include incorporating learners’ interests and prior experience, explaining the grouping rationale to students, and providing feedback on evolving collaboration patterns.
